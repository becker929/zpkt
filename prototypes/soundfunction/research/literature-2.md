## 6. Sidechain ducking and low-frequency/tactile perception

**International Organization for Standardization, ISO 226:2003, "Acoustics — Normal equal-loudness-level contours."** Tier 3.
https://www.iso.org/standard/34222.html (abstract page only, standard body paywalled) ;
https://sengpielaudio.com/Acoustics226-2003.pdf (title/metadata confirmed, body is scanned images, not machine-readable) ;
https://lawrenceyule.com/2024/06/07/equal-loudness-contours-iso-226-2003/ (opened, secondary explainer with numeric detail)
The standard's equal-loudness contours show that a sub-bass tone needs far more SPL than a 1 kHz tone to be perceived as equally loud, and that the gap between low-frequency and mid-frequency sensitivity shrinks at high SPL — the curves "flatten" as playback gets louder, which is the regime a club system operates in. This is the textbook basis for why sub/low-band energy shares measured off a waveform do not translate directly to perceived loudness or weight without knowing playback level. For our band-energy-share and sub-weight measures: a fixed sub-band energy share means something different perceptually at bedroom-monitor level than at club SPL, so any claim about "sub weight" as a role cue should flag that our corpus has no playback-level anchor.

**Hove, M.J., Martinez, S.A., Stupacher, J. (2020), "Feel the Bass: Music Presented to Tactile and Auditory Modalities Increases Aesthetic Appreciation and Body Movement," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(6):1137–1147.** Tier 2 (abstract/summary-level access only — APA PsycNet and Ovid full text were paywalled).
https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Feel-the-bass:-Music-presented-to-tactile-and-and-Hove-Martinez/09d1fdd717335a59e825bda656daaa35b40ef021 ;
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31697113/ ; https://www.ovid.com/journals/jepge/fulltext/10.1037/xge0000708~feel-the-bass-music-presented-to-tactile-and-auditory (402, paywalled)
Adding felt bass (a subwoofer or vibrotactile backpack) to the same auditory stimulus produced more forceful tapping, more spontaneous body movement, and higher ratings of both groove and enjoyment than audio alone, across two experiments (earphones+subwoofer; earphones+tactile backpack). This is direct experimental evidence that sub-bass content does causal work on the felt sense of groove that is separable from what the ear alone registers. For our measures: sub-band share and pump depth on the bass stem are proxies for a channel (touch) our audio-only pipeline cannot access at all, so a track that reads as "sub-light" acoustically could still be felt as heavy on a real system — a ceiling on what our band-share numbers can explain about perceived weight.

**Merchel, S. & Altinsoy, M.E. (2018), "Auditory-Tactile Experience of Music," in *Musical Haptics*, Springer, pp.123–148.** Tier 2/3 (abstract/summary only — full chapter behind a Springer login wall).
https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-58316-7_7 (redirected to idp.springer.com login; not opened past the abstract) ;
https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Auditory-Tactile-Experience-of-Music-Merchel-Altinsoy/2c2f1a6dbcf9653881fb4f00cacbd54ab91102a1
Reviews the authors' own psychophysical experiments on how audio-induced body vibration is integrated with heard sound, varying frequency, intensity and timing of the vibration signal separately from the audio to find what improves perceived music/concert quality. The chapter's throughline — that low-frequency vibration is processed as part of the same multimodal percept as the sound, not as noise — reinforces Hove et al. above from a production/reproduction angle rather than a listener-response one. For our measures: it is a second, independent line pointing at the same gap — sub-band share is a spectral proxy standing in for a felt quantity our pipeline cannot measure directly.

**Remache-Vinueza, B., Trujillo-León, A., Vidal-Verdú, F. (2025), "Vibrotactile stimulus duration threshold for perception of pulse to vibration transition," *Scientific Reports* 15:5057.** Tier 1/2.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11814341/ ; https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-85778-6
Across 40, 80, 150, 250, 300 and 590 Hz, 35 participants needed a consistent ~25–30 ms of stimulus before a tactile pulse was perceived as a continuous vibration rather than a discrete tap — a duration threshold that barely varies with frequency, unlike hearing, where higher frequencies resolve into a tone much faster. Applied to a rumble or sub-bass hit gated by sidechain compression, a duck-and-release cycle shorter than ~30 ms would read on the body as a series of taps rather than a hum even at 40–80 Hz. For our measures: this gives a candidate lower bound for pump return time and for the "sustain share after 50 ms" measure to be treated as tactically continuous versus percussive — 264–268 ms (our own measured median return time in parts two/three) is comfortably in the "vibration" regime, but this threshold matters for any future measure of individual duck cycles within a rolling bassline.

**Danielsen, A., Johansson, M., Brøvig, R., Sandvik, B., Bøhler, K.K. (2023), "Shaping rhythm: timing and sound in five groove-based genres," *Popular Music* 42(1):20–41.** Tier 2.
https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/popular-music/article/shaping-rhythm-timing-and-sound-in-five-groovebased-genres/BBC410F9849DB982AEBFACEA14D38F32
Compares EDM, hip-hop, jazz, samba and Scandinavian fiddle music and finds genre-specific divisions of labor between timing and sound in the construction of groove: EDM producers interviewed described shaping groove chiefly through sidechaining and envelope manipulation of *sounds*, not through microtiming, in contrast to jazz (collective timing precision, sound secondary) or samba (microrhythmic displacement). This is a second, independent academic confirmation — after Brøvig-Hanssen et al. (2020), already in `literature.md` — that ducking envelopes are the primary groove-shaping lever in this music, from a comparative rather than single-genre study. For our measures: it is direct support for treating pump depth/return as a first-class structural measure on a par with rhythmic-position measures, rather than a mix-hygiene detail, and for interpreting a change in pump shape (not just onset timing) as a legitimate "role change" signal at transitions.

**Bechtold, T.A., Jerjen, R., Hoesl, F., Kilchenmann, L., Senn, O. (2025), "The Lucerne Groove Library: An audio stimulus corpus of 444 short Western popular music drum and bass patterns with behavioural, structural, and audio measurements," *Behavior Research Methods* (OSF preprint).** Tier 2 (preprint of a peer-reviewed methods paper).
https://sciety.org/articles/activity/10.31219/osf.io/g7xs5_v1 ; https://osf.io/preprints/osf/g7xs5_v1 (landing page only, PDF not machine-readable in this session)
A 444-pattern corpus of drum-and-bass loops (rock/funk/disco/soul, not EDM) paired with tempo, event-density and audio measurements including "low-frequency sub-band flux" alongside behavioural urge-to-move ratings — the same combination of structural + spectral + behavioural measurement our own corpus attempts, applied to a different repertoire. It is a peer-reviewed precedent for treating a sub-band flux measure (rate of change of low-band energy, not just its share) as a groove-relevant descriptor in its own right. For our measures: it suggests adding sub-band flux (not just sub-band share, and not just pump depth measured on-beat) as a companion measure — a track with heavy but static sub energy and a track with heavy, rapidly modulated (pumping) sub energy would have identical band-share numbers but very different flux, and flux may be what actually drives the felt "weight" role.

## 7. Kick drum perception: pitch, distortion, transient, masking

**Gordon, J.W. (1987), "The perceptual attack time of musical tones," *JASA* 82(1):88–105.** Tier 1/2 (paywalled at the publisher and at PubMed's cookie wall; cited from the Wikipedia summary of its findings, which is itself sourced to the paper).
https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/82/1/88/795005/The-perceptual-attack-time-of-musical (403, abstract page only) ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceptual_attack_time (opened, summarizes the paper's findings)
Defines perceptual attack time (PAT) — when a tone's attack is *heard* to happen, as distinct from its physical onset — and reports it depends on both rise time and listening level; highly percussive sounds (a closed hi-hat) have a PAT of a few milliseconds, while slow-attack tones (bowed violin) can have a PAT 50–100 ms after the physical onset. This is the perceptual-timing counterpart to the purely acoustic "rise time" descriptors already in `literature.md` (Lakatos 2000, Peeters 2011): two sounds with the same measured rise time can still land at different perceived instants if loudness differs. For our measures: rise time as we compute it is a physical-signal quantity; PAT theory implies that a soft, slow-building kick and a loud, fast one with the same nominal rise time may not "land on the beat" equally well perceptually, which bears directly on how we should interpret rise time next to attack-position-in-bar.

**Baltes, F., Chemnitz, L., Lange, E.B. (2023), "Pleasantness of nonlinear distortion in isolated triads of synthetic timbre," *JASA* 154(2):1028–1040.** Tier 2.
https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/en/works/ld8OaYnl/ (opened, full abstract) ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37584950/ (abstract page)
Symmetric and asymmetric nonlinear distortion applied to synthetic triads reduced rated pleasantness overall — more so for minor than major chords and more so for asymmetric than symmetric distortion — but pleasantness correlated with roughness variability, spectral centroid and intensity rather than distortion type alone, and critically, participants who reported preferring electronic music showed smaller drops in enjoyment from distortion than other listeners. This is a rare quantitative, listener-tested link between a production choice (distortion symmetry/amount) and a perceptual outcome (pleasantness), with a built-in genre-preference moderator that applies almost by definition to a hard-techno-listening population. For our measures: it supports treating crest factor and a companion roughness/distortion-symmetry descriptor as a joint measure rather than crest factor alone, and it is a caution that a hard-techno-tuned listener's pleasantness function for distorted kicks is measurably different from a general population's — external distortion-pleasantness norms should not be imported unmodified.

**Micheyl, C., Divis, K., Wrobleski, D.M., Oxenham, A.J. (2010), "Does fundamental-frequency discrimination measure virtual pitch discrimination?" *JASA* 128(4):1930–1942.** Tier 2.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2981110/
Shows that discrimination of a complex tone's fundamental is really discrimination of its inferred ("virtual"/residue) pitch rather than of any single frequency component, and that this virtual pitch becomes ambiguous — multiple candidate pitches, larger discrimination thresholds — once the harmonic structure is inharmonic or sparse, as in short, noisy, low-frequency transients. A distorted, pitch-enveloped kick with a fast-decaying, harmonically messy attack is close to the inharmonic case this paper studies. For our measures: "landing pitch at 100 ms" reports a single Hz value, but this paper implies that value should be paired with a pitch-salience/confidence score, since a kick whose fundamental is weak or masked by distortion products may not have a well-defined landing pitch for a listener even where our pitch tracker confidently reports one.

**Zwicker, E. & Fastl, H. (1990/1999), *Psychoacoustics: Facts and Models*, Springer.** Tier 3.
https://archive.org/details/psychoacousticsf0000zwic
The standard reference for masking, critical bands, roughness and pitch strength: dedicated chapters formalize simultaneous and temporal masking (a louder, spectrally close sound raises the threshold for a quieter one, more so below ~500 Hz where critical bands are wide in absolute Hz terms), and define roughness as driven by the depth and rate of amplitude modulation within a critical band. Both mechanisms bear directly on kick/bass coexistence: two 4-on-the-floor low-frequency events sharing a bar-length duty cycle sit inside the same wide low-frequency critical bands, so their mutual masking is stronger than equivalent-bandwidth high-frequency sounds, and the roughness produced by kick-rate amplitude modulation of a sustained bass or rumble is exactly what the sidechain "pump" sounds like at short release times. For our measures: it gives the psychoacoustic mechanism (critical-band masking, low-frequency-biased) underneath our observed masking between kick sub-band energy and bass-stem pump depth, and suggests that at short pump-return times the "pump" itself becomes a roughness cue rather than a pure loudness-envelope cue — a possible reason very short return times read as "aggressive" rather than merely "loud."

**Credland, C., "Kick Drum Theory," Credland Audio (BigKick documentation).** Tier 4 (named practitioner — plugin developer specializing in kick-drum synthesis).
https://www.credland.net/kick-drum-theory.html
States that kick drums "sound best" with a body in roughly the 40–65 Hz range — below it the drum loses depth, above it "stops sounding very much like a kick" — and separately identifies the pitch-envelope (decay of the tone from a higher starting pitch down to that resting frequency), the attack/click component (anywhere from filtered noise to a distorted tom or snare), and the tail length as the four independently tunable design axes. The 40–65 Hz resting-frequency band this practitioner names for a "kick" sits directly below the 51–64 Hz interquartile range our own corpus measured for real kicks' 100 ms landing pitch (part three), suggesting our real-world median is already at the bright edge of what this practitioner would call kick territory and closer to what he'd call "loses the thud" if pushed further up. For our measures: this is a named-practitioner target range to benchmark landing pitch against, and it explicitly separates "pitch envelope shape" from "resting pitch," implying our landing-pitch-at-100ms measure should ideally be reported alongside a pitch-envelope-slope measure rather than as a single settled value.

## 8. Downbeat tracking, DJ-mix structure, and cue points in electronic dance music

**Hockman, J.A., Davies, M.E.P., Fujinaga, I. (2012), "One in the Jungle: Downbeat Detection in Hardcore, Jungle, and Drum and Bass," ISMIR 2012, pp.169–174.** Tier 2.
https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/169_ISMIR_2012.pdf (opened and read in full)
On 206 excerpts of fast, breakbeat-driven electronic dance genres, four general-purpose downbeat trackers scored 7–51% accuracy while a style-specific model built around a low-frequency-focused onset-detection function (summing the lowest ERB sub-bands, reasoning that "drum types most associated with downbeats are kick drums") reached 74.7%, with most remaining errors landing on beat 3 rather than the true downbeat — evidence that these genres' phrase symmetry (beats 1 and 3 both often carrying kicks) confuses even a specialized model. This is a direct, quantitative demonstration that a low-frequency-focused onset function is what makes downbeat detection tractable in kick-driven electronic genres, and that generalized (harmonic-change-based) trackers fail badly on them. For our measures: it validates the logic behind measuring "position of attacks in the 16-step bar" from sub-band energy rather than full-spectrum onsets, and its beat-1/beat-3 confusion is a concrete failure mode to check for in our own kick-attack-position and backbeat-asymmetry statistics — our 50%-of-attacks-on-beat figure (part two) should be examined for a similar 1-vs-3 split before being treated as clean downbeat evidence.

**Zehren, M., Alunno, M., Bientinesi, P. (2020), "Automatic Detection of Cue Points for DJ Mixing."** Tier 2.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.08411
Building general rules for DJ "switch points" from interviews with professional DJs and a novelty-based feature analysis (rhythmic density, loudness, timbre, harmony), the authors report that roughly 96% of automatically generated cue points were judged suitable for DJ mixing against a manually annotated benchmark dataset. This predates and is methodologically simpler than the same first author's 2024 switch-point paper already in `literature.md`, but is useful in its own right for naming which feature families (rhythmic density, loudness, timbral novelty, harmonic novelty) DJs treat as jointly diagnostic of a mixable boundary. For our measures: it supports treating "kick-free bars" and "phrase returns" as compound rather than single-feature events — a good transition point in the source material combines a rhythmic-density drop with a loudness/timbral shift, which is closer to what a kick-free-bar-plus-brightness-change signature would need to look like to count as a genuine structural boundary rather than noise.

**Argüello, G., Lanzendörfer, L.A., Wattenhofer, R. (2024), "Cue Point Estimation using Object Detection," ISMIR 2024.** Tier 2.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.06823
Introduces EDM-CUE, a dataset of 4,710 EDM tracks (95–190 BPM) with tempo, beat and downbeat grids plus 21,461 expert-DJ-placed cue points (~4.6 per track) drawn from professional DJs' rekordbox libraries, and frames cue-point estimation as object detection with a fine-tuned pre-trained transformer, reporting positional accuracy that respects EDM phrasing conventions without hand-built musical features. The dataset's scale (35× the prior public benchmark) and its direct sourcing from working DJs' actual cue placements make it the most concrete available proxy for "where a professional listener marks structurally meaningful points" in EDM generally. For our measures: cross-referencing our phrase-return statistic (8-bar returns in 29% of cases vs 12% random, per part two) against EDM-CUE's cue-point spacing distribution — if it were filtered to the 140–160 BPM band — would let us check whether our measured phrase grid actually coincides with where DJs mix, rather than only with where a novelty detector fires; this is listed as a gap below since that BPM-filtered comparison has not been done.

**Heydari, M., Cwitkowitz, F., Duan, Z. (2021), "BeatNet: CRNN and Particle Filtering for Online Joint Beat, Downbeat and Meter Tracking," ISMIR 2021.** Tier 2.
https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/10.5334/tismir.198 (fetched the related BeatNet+ TISMIR article, which restates BeatNet's design and reports genre-conditioned results) ;
https://github.com/mjhydri/BeatNet
A CRNN computes beat/downbeat activations causally from a spectrogram, and a cascaded particle filter turns these into online beat, downbeat, tempo and meter estimates without needing a primed time signature; on GTZAN, downbeat F1 is markedly lower than beat F1 (46.7% vs 75.4%), and the successor BeatNet+ paper notes its best genre-conditioned performance comes on Disco and Hip-Hop, attributed to strong, regular percussive cues, without separately reporting techno or hard-dance results. The persistent beat/downbeat accuracy gap in a general-purpose, non-genre-specific tracker is the same phenomenon Hockman et al. (2012) diagnosed and fixed for breakbeat genres by going low-frequency-specific — no equivalent style-specific downbeat tracker exists yet for 4/4 kick-driven techno. For our measures: it is further evidence that a generic MIR downbeat tracker cannot be trusted as ground truth for our attack-position-in-bar or phrase-return measures in this repertoire, reinforcing our own reliance on kick-stem-level attack detection rather than a general beat tracker.

## 9. Timbre embeddings for drum sounds and EDM subgenre classification

**Hsu, W-H., Chen, B-Y., Yang, Y-H. (2021), "Deep Learning Based EDM Subgenre Classification using Mel-Spectrogram and Tempogram Features."** Tier 2 (arXiv preprint; not confirmed peer-reviewed).
https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.08862
On 75,000 tracks spanning 30 EDM subgenres, adding Fourier and autocorrelation tempogram features to a mel-spectrogram CNN+ResNet classifier improved on a prior tree-based state of the art, implying tempo *shape* (not just BPM) carries subgenre-discriminating information beyond spectral timbre alone. The paper does not report a per-genre confusion matrix in any material we could access, so it is not possible to say from this source alone what specifically separates techno from hard techno, schranz or industrial techno within its 30-way taxonomy. For our measures: it suggests that a tempo/onset-density-shape descriptor (distinct from raw BPM) alongside our existing spectral measures could help distinguish hard-techno-specific patterns (e.g., a straighter, less swung sixteenth-note hat grid) from adjacent subgenres, which our current feature set does not explicitly capture.

**Lattner, S. (2022), "SampleMatch: Drum Sample Retrieval by Musical Context," ISMIR 2022.** Tier 2.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.01141
Uses contrastive learning to score how well a candidate drum one-shot fits an existing (possibly incomplete) mix, trained to rank samples originating from the same original song above others, and validates the learned scoring function against both human listening-test ratings and objective metrics. This demonstrates that "fit" between a drum sound and its musical context is a learnable, quantifiable quantity distinct from the sound's identity alone — a kick or rumble can be acoustically similar to another yet fit a given track's spectral/rhythmic context differently. For our measures: it suggests a possible refinement beyond our current role classifier — a learned context-fit score between a kick/rumble candidate and its track, which could help explain part of the real-stem classification gap (29% vs 96% synthetic accuracy, part two) if producers are choosing samples partly for contextual fit rather than for hitting canonical role-defining acoustic targets.

**Delgado, A., Saitis, C., Benetos, E., Sandler, M. (2022), "Deep Conditional Representation Learning for Drum Sample Retrieval by Vocalisation," Interspeech 2022.** Tier 2.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04651
Conditional autoencoders trained to retrieve drum samples from vocal imitations perform best when conditioned on both sound-type (drum vs. imitation) and drum-type (kick/snare/open-hat/closed-hat) labels, and the authors find large individual differences between people's vocal imitation styles, suggesting a single learned timbre space does not capture how differently people conceptualize "kick-ness" or "hat-ness." This is indirect evidence that even basic drum-role categories (kick vs. hat) are not acoustically monolithic targets even for standard drum kits, well before adding techno-specific processing chains. For our measures: it is a caution for our ten-role classification scheme — if listeners' internal category boundaries for even standard kick/snare/hat sounds are this individually variable, our fixed role labels (pulse, weight, drive, backbeat, hook, space/change) should be expected to have soft, not hard, acoustic boundaries, consistent with our own real-sample classification accuracy being far below synthetic.

## 10. Musicology and ethnography of hard/industrial techno; producer-facing rumble and layering practice

**Robin, G. (2024), "Berghain, Techno and the Body Factory: Ethnography of a Club's Regulars," *Dancecult: Journal of Electronic Dance Music Culture* 16(1).** Tier 2/3.
https://dj.dancecult.net/index.php/dancecult/article/view/1299
An ethnographic study of Berghain's regulars, drawing on interviews and participant observation, that frames the club as a ritual space (compared to Bacchanalian rites) where a "code of silence" and anonymity license a collective, bodily transformation of identity — the venue most associated internationally with the industrial/hard-techno sound this project studies. The ethnography is about social and bodily experience rather than acoustic detail, so it does not itself quantify sound; it is included as the most current peer-reviewed scholarly source specifically on the scene our corpus's music comes from. For our measures: it is a reminder that the "role" categories we measure acoustically (pulse, weight, drive, hook) exist inside a listening context — extended, repetitive, high-SPL, identity-altering — that our short (64-second, one-third-in) excerpts and audio-only measurements cannot represent, which bears on how far our findings about e.g. kick-free-bar frequency generalize to the full club experience the genre is designed for.

**Attack Magazine, "Rolling Techno" (Beat Dissected).** Tier 5 (industry tutorial; artists Andre Crom, Perc, Setaoc Mass and Tommy Four Seven are named as stylistic reference, not quoted directly).
https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/rolling-techno/
Describes a two-kick "pendulum" technique (a primary 909-style kick plus a quieter ghost kick before beats two and four) plus filtered-reverb sustain and either a resonant filter or narrow-Q EQ boost at the kick's root pitch to reinforce low-end character without relying purely on reverb tail. This is a second, independent practitioner description (after the "Dark Techno Rumble" and MusicRadar/Perc pieces already in `literature.md`) of low end being built from more than one kick-like event per bar rather than a single hit, which our beat-level measures would currently read as a single "kick" attack. For our measures: a ghost kick placed just before beats two and four would register in our 16-step attack-position histogram as an off-grid low-band attack, which our current pipeline may either discard as noise or misread as syncopation — worth checking whether any of the "kick-free bars" or attack-position anomalies in our data are actually ghost-kick patterns like this one.

**Attack Magazine, "Sculpting Warehouse-Style Rolling Techno Basslines."** Tier 5 (industry tutorial).
https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/tutorials/warehouse-rolling-techno-bass/
Lays out a four-layer low end for warehouse/Berghain-style techno — a pure sub layer (lowpassed ~80 Hz, transient-shaped, mildly overdriven), a mid-bass "rolling" layer (65–350 Hz band, sidechained to duck under the kick), a delayed percussive kick layer, and the main four-on-the-floor kick (notched around 98 Hz to leave room for the bassline) — explicitly framed as avoiding the frequency masking that "undermines home productions." This gives a concrete, named architecture for exactly the kind of layered kick/sub/bassline stack our part-three pump-depth measurements (mix −10 dB, bass-on-beat −11 dB, bass-on-kick-onset −12 dB) are trying to characterize from the outside, without stem access to which of these four layers is doing what. For our measures: it suggests our single "bass stem" pump-depth measure is likely averaging across at least two differently-ducked layers (a heavily sidechained rolling bassline and a comparatively untouched sub), which could explain some of the spread around our measured medians and is a candidate reason real-stem classification (part two/three) underperforms synthetic.

**MusicRadar, "8 pro techno producers explain how they create their tracks."** Tier 4 (named practitioners: Tom Hades, Matador, Secret Cinema, Paranoid London, Luke Slater, Wehbba, BEC, Tomy DeClerque).
https://www.musicradar.com/music-tech/record-everything-all-the-time-and-keep-it-all-8-pro-techno-producers-explain-how-they-create-their-tracks
Tom Hades describes building a kick that "doubles up as a sub bass" through processing and then sidechaining that sub-bass element to duck under the same kick that generated it — i.e., the kick and its sub extension are the same processed source rather than two independently designed layers — while other producers describe distortion-heavy, multi-hardware kick shaping (Luke Slater's Culture Vulture turning a 909 into "booming techno bass") and minimal arrangements (BEC's 15-element track including three separate kick sounds). Tom Hades's account in particular complicates a clean kick-vs-rumble separation: if the rumble is a processed derivative of the kick signal itself, "kick" and "weight" are not acoustically independent sound sources but two views of one processing chain. For our measures: this is a plausible, named-practitioner explanation for why our real-stem role classifier drops to 29% accuracy (part two) where synthetic sounds reach 96% — synthetic training examples assume kick and rumble are separately generated, while real production practice sometimes derives one from the other, so the two "roles" share acoustic DNA that a role classifier trained on independent synthetic sources cannot anticipate.

## 11. Loudness, dynamic range and mastering norms

**Deruty, E. & Tardieu, D. (2014), "About Dynamic Processing in Mainstream Music," *Journal of the Audio Engineering Society* 62(1/2):42–55.** Tier 2.
https://aes2.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=17085
Analyzing a decades-spanning corpus of mainstream recordings, the authors find that a shift from "high-fidelity and transparency" (1967–1984) to "low-fidelity and transient degradation" (1984–2004) reduced transient prominence but left macrodynamics (the peak-to-average ratio across a whole track) largely intact, and argue that listener "ear fatigue" attributed to the loudness war more plausibly comes from degraded microdynamics (transient detail) than from any real loss of large-scale dynamic range. This separates two things our single crest-factor measure conflates: a track-level peak/RMS ratio (macrodynamics) and the fine transient shape of individual hits (microdynamics, closer to our rise-time and decay measures). For our measures: it implies crest factor alone may not distinguish a track that is "heavily limited but still transient-rich" from one that is "heavily limited and transient-smeared" — pairing crest factor with rise-time-at-the-mix-level, not just per-stem, could better capture the mastering-stage compression our current measures are not designed to see.

**Taylor, R.W. (2017), "Hyper-compression in Music Production; Agency, Structure and the Myth that 'Louder is Better'," *Journal on the Art of Record Production* 11.** Tier 2/3.
https://www.arpjournal.com/asarpwp/hyper-compression-in-music-production-agency-structure-and-the-myth-that-louder-is-better/
Argues that hyper-compression became a structural requirement of commercial music production (a "prisoner's dilemma" among producers and labels) rather than a purely aesthetic choice, building on the author's earlier listener-preference study (with Martens, AES 2014) that a moderate amount of compression is preferred but preference plateaus or reverses beyond a threshold tied to a track's original crest factor. For our measures: it is a sociological rather than acoustic explanation for why our corpus's crest-factor and mastering-loudness numbers (untested for LUFS in our published pages) might cluster near an industry-wide loudness target regardless of a given track's actual arrangement, which is a reason not to read a low measured crest factor as evidence about arrangement or sound design without also checking mastering-stage loudness.

**Stewart, I. (2025), "A LUFS and tonal analysis of 10 top-charting dance and electronic songs," iZotope.** Tier 5 (industry/vendor blog, named author, data reported directly).

Ten Billboard Hot Dance/Electronic chart tracks measured −6.2 to −11.3 LUFS integrated (mean −8.3 LUFS) and −4.5 to −8.6 LUFS short-term peak, with the author's headline point that the loudest track in the sample was not the most commercially successful — "good and loud don't always go hand in hand." This is a small, non-random, chart-pop-leaning sample (Marshmello, Anyma, Justice, etc., not underground hard techno), but it is a directly measured, opened data point for what "loud" means in mainstream electronic music right now, useful as an external anchor since our published pages do not report LUFS for our own corpus at all. For our measures: it flags integrated LUFS and short-term peak as measures entirely absent from our current pipeline, and suggests that if we added them, hard techno's underground, club-first mastering norm should be checked against — and likely sits louder than — this chart-pop baseline rather than assumed to match it.

### (b) How this sweep bears on the published pages

- **Supports** the central claim of part two ("three things do most of the work: brightness, sub weight, how long a sound lasts") being framed around *sound* rather than timing: Danielsen et al. (2023) independently find, across a comparative study of five groove genres, that EDM producers build groove chiefly through sidechaining and envelope manipulation of sounds rather than microtiming — the opposite emphasis from jazz or samba in their sample.
- **Supports** part three's ducking numbers (mix −10 dB / bass-on-beat −11 dB / bass-on-kick-onset −12 dB, 268 ms recovery, 144/149 tracks ducking >3 dB): this is close to what a rolling-bassline architecture with a separately sidechained mid-bass layer, as described in the Attack Magazine "Warehouse Bass" tutorial, would produce when measured as a single averaged bass stem — the tutorial's four-layer structure is a plausible mechanism behind the numbers rather than just a confirmation of them.
- **Complicates** the clean kick/rumble/bass role separation implicit in the classifier (part one/two): Tom Hades's account (MusicRadar) of a kick "doubling up as a sub bass" through processing, and the Attack Magazine warehouse-bass layering, both describe production practice where "kick" and "weight" are not independently sourced — which is a concrete, named-practitioner explanation for why real-stem classification accuracy (29%, part two/three) is so far below synthetic (96%): the synthetic training data assumes independence the real production chain does not have.
- **Extends but does not directly test** the downbeat/attack-position findings (part one/two: 25/26 tracks landing the primary sub-band attack on-beat; ~50% of sub-band attacks on the four beats vs. 25% chance): Hockman, Davies & Fujinaga (2012) validate the general strategy (low-frequency-focused onset detection for downbeat finding in kick-driven electronic genres) but also document a specific beat-1-vs-beat-3 confusion in that strategy that our own published analysis does not report checking for.
- **Flags an unaddressed variable, not a contradiction**: the tactile/vibrotactile literature (Hove et al. 2020; Merchel & Altinsoy 2018; Remache-Vinueza et al. 2025) and the equal-loudness standard (ISO 226:2003) together imply that "sub weight" as felt in a club depends on absolute playback level in a way our band-energy-share measures, taken from files with no stated playback-level anchor, cannot capture — this qualifies rather than undermines the sub-band-share findings in parts one and two.
- **Names a measurement gap** in all three published pages: none report LUFS, integrated loudness, or any mastering-stage loudness measure, despite mastering norms being a live, quantifiable variable in this genre (Stewart 2025; Deruty & Tardieu 2014; Taylor 2017).

### (c) Remaining gaps after this sweep

- Still no acoustic study of individually-produced post-2018 hard/industrial techno sound design at the sound level; the closest sources remain adjacent (general EDM MIR, general drum psychoacoustics, or ethnography of the scene without acoustic measurement).
- No study connects distorted, pitch-enveloped kick drums specifically (as opposed to synthetic complex tones) to virtual-pitch salience — Micheyl et al. (2010) is the closest available mechanism, but untested on real kicks.
- No drum-sample embedding or retrieval model (SampleMatch, vocalisation-based retrieval, or the EDM subgenre classifier) has been trained or evaluated on distorted techno kicks, rumbles, or hardgroove/schranz percussion; the datasets behind all of them remain standard-kit or broad-EDM material.
- No source, in this sweep or the first, reports a per-genre breakdown separating hard techno / schranz / industrial techno from techno or hardcore within a subgenre classifier — the closest (Hsu, Chen & Yang 2021) works at a 30-subgenre scale without a published confusion matrix we could access.
- EDM-CUE's cue-point spacing statistics have not been filtered to the 140–160 BPM band and compared against our own phrase-return measurements — a concrete, doable cross-check that remains undone.
- No peer-reviewed loudness/LUFS study targets hard or industrial techno specifically; both sources found (Stewart 2025, Deruty & Tardieu 2014) are chart-pop or general-mainstream, not underground club music, and our own published pages do not measure LUFS at all.
- The one paper that most directly modeled sidechain compression as groove-construction in EDM by name — Hasnan, H.H. & Abd Wahab, A.F., "Sidechain Compression and the Sonic Construction of Groove," *VISITSILP-Journal of Arts* (SWU, Thailand) — could not be opened: the publisher's server returned a 503 through the tool and a TLS certificate error via direct download, so it is not cited above despite being found and looking directly on-topic; a future session with different network access should retry it.
